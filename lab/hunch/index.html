Every guess starts as a hunch. A clue shows up, and your hunch gets smarter. That's the whole idea behind one of the most useful pieces of math ever invented — you're about to use the real thing, not a cartoon of it, to crack a case.

01 — meet the jars

Two jars, one secret

There are two jars of candy, Jar A and Jar B. Each one has its own mix of orange and blueberry candies — you can see exactly what's in each jar, that part's no secret. Then a coin gets flipped where you can't see it: heads means a candy will come from Jar A, tails means Jar B. That's the secret. Before anything else happens, your best guess has to be a plain 50/50 — that's your starting hunch.

03 — do the math

Why the hunch moved

Draw a candy to see this fill in. Here's the trick: imagine the case happened 100 times instead of once, and count how many of those 100 imaginary cases match what you actually saw.

The top rows are the 100 imaginary cases where the coin picked Jar A; the bottom rows are where it picked Jar B. Every dot is colored by whichever candy that imaginary case would have pulled. The ones with a ring around them are the only ones that match your actual clue — and most of the ringed dots sit in whichever jar is more likely.

How this actually works — the formula, and its real name

Everything above is Bayes' theorem, just without the notation. Reverend Thomas Bayes worked out this exact idea in the 1740s — how to turn a starting guess into a smarter one once evidence shows up — and it's now behind things like spam filters, medical tests, and weather forecasts (see "Why this matters," below). If you ever see it written in a textbook, here's the dictionary between the words used above and the words a textbook uses:

Plain word (used above)Textbook nameFormal symbol
Starting hunchPriorP(A)
How likely your clue is, if it really is Jar ALikelihoodP(clue | A)
How likely your clue is at all, either jarEvidenceP(clue)
Updated hunchPosteriorP(A | clue)

Put together, that's Bayes' theorem itself:

P(A | clue) = P(clue | A) × P(A) ÷ P(clue)

The 100-dot picture and this formula compute the exact same number — the picture just lets you count it instead of multiplying fractions. That's also exactly what this page does under the hood every time you draw a candy: no rounding, no simulation, the real formula run on the real numbers from your jars.

05 — why this matters

You just did something doctors, weather forecasters, and spam filters do every day

Spam filters start with a hunch about whether an email is junk, then update it word by word — "free," "prize," and "click here" are all clues, the same way an orange candy was a clue about which jar it came from.

Weather forecasters start with a hunch based on the season and location, then update it as new radar and satellite readings — new clues — come in through the day.

Doctors start with a hunch based on how common a sickness is, then update it once a test result — a clue, and not always a perfectly certain one — comes back.

None of these are guessing games with candy jars, but every one of them is the exact same move you just made four times: start with a hunch, let a real clue change it, end up smarter than when you started.
