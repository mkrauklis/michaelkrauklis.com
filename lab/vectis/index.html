Vectis

Pick two words — anything, "formal" and "playful," "chaotic" and "calm." Each one becomes an axis. Drop in words, phrases, or your own photos, and each one gets placed on the map based on nothing but how close it sits to each of your two words. There's no dataset, no pretrained categories — just one repeated operation, similarity to a word you chose, done twice per item.

An embedding is just a list of numbers a model uses to stand in for what something means — a word, a phrase, a photo — placed so that things which mean similar things end up with similar numbers. Pick two things as your axes below, and everything else you add gets plotted by how similar it is to each one, turning that list of numbers into an actual map you can see and click around in. Nothing you type or upload ever leaves your browser; the model itself, how the plotting math works, and two hands-on demos are in "how this actually works," below the tool.

Tips for picking axis words

Works best with two different topics ("ocean" / "mountain", "food" / "technology") rather than opposite personality traits or physical properties ("calm" / "chaotic", "hairy" / "safe") — CLIP's raw text similarity is much better at recognizing what something is about than judging whether it literally has a trait. More on why below the tool.

3. what's on the map

Click any point (or its line back to the center) to see how it got there, and what else on the map it's most like.

Heads up: CLIP's raw similarity numbers for photos sit in a noticeably different range than its numbers for words (a known quirk called the "modality gap"). This page corrects for it by estimating the gap between photos and words currently on your map and shifting photo scores to compensate, which is enough to plot both together sensibly — but with only a photo or two to estimate from, the correction is an approximation, not a lab-grade fix, so treat exact photo placement with a little extra skepticism.

How this actually works — the geometry, and two things to try by hand

Everything on this page runs a small vision-and-language model called CLIP entirely in your browser tab. The one exception, worth being upfront about: the very first time you use this page, your browser downloads the model's weights (a few tens of megabytes) from a public model host, the same way it would download a font or a script. That's the model learning nothing from you — it's already finished learning, from millions of captioned photos, before it ever reached your machine. After that first download your browser caches it, and every photo or phrase you plot from then on never leaves this tab. CLIP maps images and text into the same space of numbers, trained so that a photo of a dog and the word "dog" land near each other in that space — which is what makes it possible to compare a typed word against an uploaded photo at all.

A word doesn't mean anything to a computer by itself — but CLIP has seen enough captioned photos that it's learned to place any word, phrase, or image as a point in a space with a few hundred dimensions, positioned so that things which mean similar things end up near each other. "Dog" and a photo of a golden retriever land close together; "dog" and "spreadsheet" land far apart. That's the entire premise of the tool above: pick two words, use them as two directions in that space, and read off how far along each direction everything else falls.

Cosine similarity is the specific way "how close" gets measured. Instead of straight-line distance, it looks at the angle between two vectors — two things pointing the same direction score 1, perpendicular scores 0, opposite directions score −1, regardless of how long either vector is. That's what the widget below lets you feel directly, with no model involved at all.

–

One deliberate limit, worth stating plainly: everything you see above — the plot, the ranked list, the network view — is computed only from the two similarity numbers per item that you can see on screen. There's no second, hidden comparison happening in the full few-hundred-dimensional space that could quietly disagree with what's plotted. That's a constraint, not a shortcut: the moment this tool could show you an answer it can't also explain, it stops teaching anything.

Privacy & technical notes

All embedding happens in a background thread in your browser using transformers.js running OpenAI's CLIP (a public, quantized ONNX build). Your words, phrases, and photos are never uploaded — the only network activity is the one-time model download, cached by your browser afterward. Refreshing the page clears everything you've plotted; nothing is saved anywhere.
