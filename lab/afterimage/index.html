Afterimage

Stare at something bright, close your eyes, and for a second it's still there — not the thing itself, just an impression of it, already fading. This is roughly that, except the thing doing the remembering is a small neural network, and the impression is exactly 256 numbers.

Upload a photo below and nothing gets stored, uploaded, or looked up anywhere — it never leaves your browser. Instead, a decoder that has never seen your photo, and never will store it, spends about a minute doing something closer to remembering than copying: searching for the shortest description of your photo that its own memory of thirteen thousand other people's photos can turn back into something recognizable. What comes out isn't your photo. It's an impression of it, filtered entirely through what the network has learned a photo can look like.

That's also, a little uncannily, close to how your own memory works — you don't store photographs in your head either, you store a compressed sketch and rebuild the rest on the fly from a lifetime of "what things generally look like." This page just makes that process slow enough to watch and small enough to hand someone else as a string of numbers or a QR code.

The mechanics, if you want them before you've even tried it: a convolutional decoder is trained once, offline, then frozen forever. Uploading your photo doesn't retrain that network — instead, a much smaller thing gets trained, live, in your browser, on the spot, using your one photo as its only lesson: 256 numbers get pushed around by gradient descent until the frozen decoder's output matches your photo as closely as it can. It's the same trick behind DeepDream and style-transfer demos: freeze the big network, train the input instead. There's more on this — architecture diagrams and all — in the "how this actually works" section further down, once you've seen it do something.

tl;dr on the jargon

A neural network is just a pile of adjustable numbers that gets tuned until it's good at some task. A CNN — convolutional neural network — is the flavor built for images: it slides small pattern-detectors across the picture, finding edges first, then shapes, then whole recognizable chunks. An autoencoder bolts two CNNs back to back — one that squeezes an image down to a short list of numbers, one that expands that list back into an image — and trains them together until the round trip barely loses anything. That short list is the latent space: not pixels, but the network's own compressed sense of what the image is. Afterimage freezes the expanding half and, per photo, searches for the short list that makes it produce your picture.

A few kinds of photos this works on — illustrative sketches, not real output; upload your own below to see the genuine thing.

Faces, pets, and landscapes with a clear subject all do well — busy group shots and tiny distant subjects hold up less well against a network that only remembers 256 numbers' worth of any of them.

The network only gets 256 numbers to describe whatever you give it — the simpler the ask, the more faithful the impression.

03 — train

Watch it learn your photo

The big network — the decoder — was already trained, once, offline, and is frozen for good. What trains now, live, right here, is much smaller: 256 numbers (and later, 454 more) getting nudged by gradient descent, over and over, until the frozen decoder's output matches your photo as closely as it can. First an instant forward pass gives those 256 numbers a rough starting point; then a few hundred training steps refine them, and a few hundred more train a small personalization on top — usually 30-120 seconds total. Every pixel below is the network's actual output at that point in training, not a lookup or a filter, and the chart tracks the error dropping step by step so you can watch the descent itself, not just its result.

Your photo landed at one specific point in that room. Every nearby point decodes to something too — a plausible image the network can imagine, blending smoothly into whatever's next to it. How this actually works — training vs. rendering, explained

There are two completely separate phases here, and only one of them ever touches your photo.

Training happened once, ahead of time, on a GPU, using 13,000 ordinary photos of animals, vehicles, and people — never yours. A pair of networks, an encoder and a decoder, learned together to compress a photo down to 256 numbers and rebuild it from just those numbers.

Rendering is what happens in your browser just now. Both networks came along, but only one of them touches your final result: the encoder runs once, instantly, purely to get the search started somewhere reasonable instead of from nothing. Every pixel of the actual answer comes from the decoder being nudged, step by step, toward your photo — a slower process, but one that measurably beats trusting the encoder's one-shot guess. The last stretch of that search also fits 454 extra numbers — a personalization layered onto the decoder's last three layers — which is why the image kept sharpening even after the main shape had already settled.

Shared — identical bytes, every browser, forever frozen
Unique to you — exists only because of your photo, computed just now

The gray blocks are actual photo pixels. Coral is the encoder — it does run in your browser now, but only once, for that instant first guess. Teal is the decoder, the thing actually doing the work: the exact same weights, unchanged, that everyone else's browser also uses. Violet is the 256-number latent vector, the "impression" your photo gets reduced to. All three of those — every gray, coral, violet, and teal pixel in this diagram — are byte-for-byte identical no matter whose browser is running it. The ★ UNIQUE TO YOU badge is the one exception: 454 numbers that exist only because of your specific photo, layered onto the decoder's last three steps, fitted live in your browser and nowhere else. It's also the only part that gets trained twice over — first the 256 numbers alone, then this head fitted on top of them. You already saw what difference it makes in the "latent only" vs. "latent + head" comparison back in the training step — that gap is entirely this one amber piece.
